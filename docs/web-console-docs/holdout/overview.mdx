# Overview

## What is a holdout

Individual experiments tell you the impact of a single change. A **holdout** tells you the
impact of everything you shipped, together.

A holdout keeps a small, randomly selected group of visitors out of the experiments it covers.
Their behaviour becomes a baseline. By comparing that baseline with visitors who are exposed to
your experiments as normal, you can measure the cumulative effect of your experimentation
programme over a period of months rather than the effect of one change at a time.

This answers questions that individual experiment results cannot:

- Do the wins we recorded across dozens of experiments add up at the aggregate level?
- Are the effects of our changes still present months after launch, or did they decay?
- Is there an interaction between our experiments that no single test would reveal?

## Tracking units and teams

Every holdout has a **tracking unit** and belongs to one or more **teams**.

The tracking unit mirrors how experiments work. An experiment that randomises on Device ID can
only be held out from a group defined by Device ID, so a holdout only covers experiments that share
its tracking unit. The tracking unit is chosen when the holdout is created and cannot be changed
afterwards.

The teams own the holdout and decide which experiments it covers: a holdout covers the experiments
of its teams and of their child teams. They also decide who can view and manage the holdout, as
described in [Permissions](#permissions).

A **general holdout** belongs to your top-level team, the team named after your organisation. Every
other team sits under it, so a general holdout covers experiments from all teams. When you create a
holdout, leaving the **Teams** field empty makes it a general holdout.

A team can only have one holdout per tracking unit. There is therefore at most one general holdout
per tracking unit, alongside at most one holdout per team. For example, you could run a general User
ID holdout for the whole organisation and a separate User ID holdout for the landing pages team, but
not two User ID holdouts for the landing pages team.

Holdouts can overlap. An experiment from the Development team, a child of Engineering, can be
covered on the same tracking unit by the Development holdout, the Engineering holdout and the
general holdout. A visitor can be held out by more than one of them.

:::info
To understand how tracking units are defined on your platform, see
[Tracking Unit](/docs/web-console-docs/experiments/creating-an-experiment#tracking-unit).
:::

## Simple and advanced holdouts

A holdout is either **simple** or **advanced**.

A **simple holdout** (the default) keeps one group of visitors out of all experiments and compares
it with everyone else. It is the best way to measure the total cumulative impact of your
experimentation programme.

An **advanced holdout** adds a second held-out group. This group is also kept out of experiments,
but it does see the features that have already been shipped to 100% of traffic. This separates two
effects which a simple holdout measures together: the impact of what you shipped, and the impact of
being exposed to experiments which were tested but not kept.

An advanced holdout needs a second held-out group of the same size, so it takes more of your
traffic. Fewer visitors remain available for experiments, which may then need to run longer to
collect enough participants for reliable results.

## How the comparison works

A holdout report compares groups of equal size. Every variant is compared with the control.

| Group | Role | What it sees | Holdout type |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Holdout** | Control | No experiments. The baseline experience. | Simple and advanced |
| **No Holdout** | Variant 1 | Experiments as normal, exactly like the rest of your population. | Simple and advanced |
| **Full on only** | Variant 2 | No experiments, but features already shipped to 100% of traffic. | Advanced only |

The **No Holdout** group is not simply "everyone else". It is a sample drawn from the full
experiment-exposed population and sized to match the holdout group. Comparing a 3% holdout against
the remaining 97% directly would put a small group next to a very large one, and the imbalance
costs you statistical power. Sampling an equal-size comparison group keeps the two sides balanced
and the comparison fair.

The relative impact of **No Holdout** therefore reads as: how much the experiment-exposed group
differs from the holdout baseline. In an advanced holdout, the relative impact of **Full on only**
reads as: how much the features you shipped, on their own, changed behaviour compared with the
baseline.

## Traffic allocation

The traffic allocation is the share of the tracking unit's traffic kept out of experiments. It can
be set between **1% and 10%**.

| Holdout type | Default allocation |
| --- | --- |
| Simple | **3%**, all of it in the holdout group |
| Advanced | **6%**, with 3% in each of the two held-out groups |

The **No Holdout** comparison group is not part of the allocation. It is sampled from the
experiment-exposed population to match the size of the holdout group.

The size of the allocation is a trade-off. A larger holdout detects smaller cumulative effects, but
it also means more visitors are kept away from the improvements you have shipped.

## Which experiments are included

Each holdout has an **experiment inclusion** setting, which decides how experiments join it. Like
the rest of the holdout's configuration, a change to this setting applies from the next period.

**Opt-out** (the default): every experiment on the holdout's tracking unit, from its teams and
their child teams, is included automatically. Individual experiments can be excluded from the
experiment form, by users with permission to exclude them. This is worth doing sparingly. Each
exclusion means the holdout group has seen that experiment after all, so the baseline drifts a
little further from "the product as it was". Reserve exclusions for cases where withholding a change
is not acceptable, such as a legal or compliance requirement, or a fix that must reach all visitors.

**Opt-in**: experimenters add their experiment to the holdout from the experiment form. Only
experiments on the holdout's tracking unit, from its teams and their child teams, can be added, and
anyone who can edit such an experiment can add it. Only opted-in experiments take part.

## Periods and resets

A holdout runs for a **duration** chosen at setup: **3 months**, **6 months**, **12 months**, or
**until stopped**.

When a fixed duration ends, the holdout can restart automatically: a new period begins immediately
with the same configuration, and visitors are randomised again from scratch. A holdout set to run
until stopped has no end date, so it runs as a single period and does not restart automatically.

Fresh randomisation matters. A visitor who was held out during one period is unlikely to be held out
in the next, which stops the same people from being excluded from your product improvements
indefinitely, and prevents the holdout group from slowly diverging into an unrepresentative
population.

Each period is an **independent dataset** with its own results. Periods are not merged or averaged.
When a period ends its results are archived and stay accessible, so you can look back at how the
cumulative impact of your experimentation changed from one period to the next.

## Reading results before the period ends

You do not have to wait for the period to end to learn something. Holdouts are analysed once per
day using **Fully Sequential testing**, so results are valid whenever you look at them and can be
checked at any point during the period.

## Metrics

The metrics used to evaluate a holdout (max 5 of them) are selected from your existing metric
catalogue, filtered to those compatible with the holdout's tracking unit. A metric defined on User
ID cannot be computed for a Device ID holdout, so it is not offered.

Because a holdout measures broad, long-run impact rather than the effect of one change, favour your
top-line business metrics over narrow, feature-specific ones.

## Permissions

Holdouts have their own permissions, separate from those for experiments. What a user can do with a
holdout depends on their relationship with the teams that own it.

### Viewing a holdout

Members of a holdout's team can view the holdout and its results. Unlike other assets, this does
not require a team role: membership is enough. Members of child teams are also members of their
parent teams, so the holdouts which cover your experiments are visible to you.

Every user is a member of your top-level team, so everyone can view the general holdout.

A holdout can also be shared with other users or teams with **Can View**.

### Managing a holdout

Managing a holdout covers creating, editing, starting, stopping, archiving and unarchiving it.

A holdout is managed by the **Team Admins** of its team. Team roles cascade down the team
hierarchy, so the Team Admins of its parent teams can manage it too. Team Contributors and Team
Viewers cannot manage a holdout: a holdout takes traffic from every experiment in its team's scope,
so managing one is closer to managing the team than to running an experiment.

Roles held at your top-level team are global roles, so the general holdout is managed by `FullAdmin`
users and by users whose global role includes holdout permissions.

A Team Admin can delegate the management of a holdout by sharing it with a user or team with
**Can Edit**.

### Adding and excluding experiments

Experiments join and leave a holdout from the experiment form.

- **Adding** an experiment to an opt-in holdout: anyone who can edit the experiment.
- **Excluding** an experiment from an opt-out holdout: the Team Admins of the holdout's team, and
  users granted the permission to exclude experiments from holdouts.

Excluding an experiment weakens the holdout's baseline, so it has its own permission. It is not part
of the permission to edit a holdout, sharing a holdout with **Can Edit** does not grant it, and
owning the experiment is not enough on its own. This stops experimenters from removing their own
experiments from a holdout.

### Holdout permissions

The following permissions can be granted to users through custom global roles.

| Permission | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Create a holdout** | Allows creating a new holdout. |
| **Edit a holdout** | Allows changing a holdout's configuration. Changes apply from the next period. |
| **Start a holdout** | Allows starting a holdout. |
| **Stop a holdout** | Allows stopping a running holdout. Stopping a holdout manually disables its automatic restart. |
| **List holdouts** | Allows viewing the list of holdouts. |
| **Get a holdout** | Allows viewing a specific holdout and its configuration. |
| **Get holdout metrics** | Allows viewing a holdout's results. |
| **Archive a holdout** | Allows archiving a holdout. |
| **Unarchive a holdout** | Allows restoring an archived holdout. |
| **Exclude an experiment from a holdout** | Allows excluding an experiment from an opt-out holdout. |
| **Holdout admin** | Grants all holdout permissions. |

Holdouts cannot be put into development, put Full on or recomputed, so there are no permissions for
those actions.

:::info
As with other assets, holdout permissions granted through a global role apply to every holdout,
whichever team owns it. Prefer the team-based model where possible. See
[Roles](/docs/web-console-docs/users-teams-permissions/roles).
:::
